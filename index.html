Overview: Features and How to Use Them

Your caravan is like a compact apartment on wheels. Here's a quick tour of the layout and the main systems so you feel at home right away.

Layout at a Glance

  • You sleep on a fixed double bed at one end of the caravan — always ready, no converting needed.
  • Next to the bed, a table pulls out from underneath. That's your eating and sitting area. When not in use, slide it back under to free up space.
  • The kitchen is along one side, with an electric cooktop, sink, and a 12V compressor fridge.
  • The bathroom has a shower and a composting toilet.
  • A 12V chraging station with USB-C ad USB connections next to seating area (always ON).
  • The floorplan image gives you the exact positions, but you'll quickly get the feel of it in person.

Kitchen

  • Cooktop: Electric (powered by 120V). It heats up like a standard household electric stove.
  • Oven: There is no oven. Use the cooktop for all your cooking, or bring a small portable appliance if you like.
  • Fridge: A 12V compressor fridge, wired directly to the 12V battery system — not through the inverter. This means it always runs off the house batteries, whether you're plugged in or not, and it's very efficient. The temperature can be adjusted with a dial inside.

Bathroom

  • Composting toilet: Very different from a regular flush toilet. It separates liquid and solid waste into two containers, with no water used for flushing. Solids break down with the help of a dry medium (coconut coir) and need emptying every few weeks. Liquids go into a separate container that needs emptying more frequently. There's a detailed how-to in the Bathroom section and a Maintenance section — it's simpler than it sounds and there's no bad smell when used correctly.
  • Shower: Hot water comes from an electric water heater. Used water drains directly outside.

Electricity: The Heart of the House

You have several power sources working together:

  • Two 12V house batteries — the main power storage. They run the lights, water pump, fridge, and anything that plugs into a 12V socket.
  • Shore power inlet — a plug on the outside to connect to a campsite's electrical pedestal. When connected, you have "endless" 120V power, and the batteries recharge.
  • Solar panels on the roof — they charge the batteries silently when the sun shines. No effort needed from you.
  • Inverter — converts 12V battery power into 120V household power for outlets. This is how you can use the electric cooktop or charge a laptop when you're not on shore power. High-draw appliances (water heater) will deplete the batteries faster — use them wisely.

Water System

  • A fresh water tank (approx. 40 gallons) holds clean water for the sink and shower. A 12V pump pressurizes the system whenever you open a tap.
  • The water heater is electric only (120V). It takes about 5–10 minutes. You can turn it on from a switch — be mindful of water and battery capacity when off-grid.
  • Wastewater from the sink goes into a bucket under the sink — empty it regularly. The shower drains directly outside.

Electricity: Inverter and Appliances

This section brings together everything you need to manage power day to day — your quick-reference "how to run the house" guide.

The Big Picture

Your power system runs on an AIMS Power inverter/charger unit. It does two jobs:

  • Inverter mode: Converts 12V battery power into 120V AC for the stove, outlets, and water heater.
  • Charger mode: When shore power is plugged in and the inverter is switched ON, it automatically recharges the batteries.

The inverter is normally kept OFF. You only switch it ON when you need 120V power (cooking, outlets) or when you're plugged into shore power and want to charge the batteries.

How the Inverter Works (the Unit Itself)

  • The unit sits under the bed, left side, with a main ON/OFF switch on its top panel and a control board/display showing status.
  • Normal state: OFF. Even when off, the fridge and 12V lights still run directly from the batteries.
  • To use 120V or charge batteries: Turn the inverter ON using the switch on the unit or the switch inside. The control board will light up.
  • After use: Turn the inverter OFF again to save battery power. The inverter itself draws some power when sitting idle, so don't leave it on continuously unless you're actively using it.

⚠️ Inverter Alarm — Important Safety

  • The inverter will light an alarm light and beep if it's dangerously overloaded.
  • If the alarm goes off, reduce your power use immediately. Turn off the stove, water heater, or any heavy device. Continuing to ignore the alarm will blow the main fuse behind the inverter, and you'll lose all 120V power.
  • Treat the alarm like a smoke detector — stop what you're doing and lighten the load.

Daily Power Sequence

When you're off-grid (no shore power):

  1. The inverter stays OFF unless you need the stove, outlets, or water heater.
  2. To cook: Turn inverter ON → switch the Stove switch ON (on the switchboard next to seating) → cook. When finished, turn the stove switch OFF, then turn the inverter OFF.
  3. To use outlets (laptop, chargers): Turn inverter ON → turn Outlets switch ON. When done, turn both OFF.

When you want to charge batteries using shore power:

  1. Plug the shore power cable into the external inlet.
  2. Turn the inverter ON (using the unit's switch). The inverter will automatically detect shore power and begin charging the batteries. Use only 30W and below power charge.
  3. Monitor charge via the SOK app. Once the batteries are well charged (above 13.0V or 80%+ on the app), you can turn the inverter OFF and unplug shore power.
  4. Never leave the inverter ON indefinitely when shore power is disconnected if you're not using it, but it's fine during a charge cycle. After charging, switch it off.

Powering Appliances Off-Grid (Battery Only)

  • The electric stove draws a lot. You can probably cook for around 1-2 hours on a full battery, but watch your levels.
  • DON'T KILL THE BATTERIES — draining them below safe levels causes permanent damage.
  • Water heater: Use sparingly off-grid. A 5–30 minute heat-up is fine, then turn it off. Best used when shore power is available but dont Stress.
  • Fridge: Always runs on 12V, very efficient. No need to touch it.

Battery Monitoring

1. Switchboard above seating: Shows battery as bars (percentage), but it's not accurate — treat it as a rough indicator only for one battery of the two.

2. SOK Battery App (recommended): Connects via Bluetooth to the batteries. Gives you real voltage and state of charge in precentage.

When to recharge: If you're off-grid and it's been cloudy, check the SOK app. When the batteries drop to 20–30%, it's time to connect to shore power. The solar panels are very efficient — in moderate sun they'll keep you charged. Only prolonged overcast skies require shore power backup.

Outlets (120V)

  • Two usable outlets: kitchen and near the bed.
  • They work only when both the inverter switch is ON AND the Outlets switch on the switchboard is ON. They do not bypass directly to shore power — they always need the inverter.
  • There's a third outlet behind the fridge dedicated to the stove. Do not touch it — the stove plugs in there.

Fridge (12V)

  • Always on, no switch. Temperature dial on the front.
  • Extremely efficient — you won't notice significant battery drain even on cloudy days. No special management needed.

Solar Panels

  • Fully automatic. They start charging whenever light hits the roof. No controls to touch, no display to check.
  • Fixed panels, no tilting needed.
  • In most daylight conditions, they'll keep you topped up.
  • Safety note: If you ever work on the batteries (which you shouldn't need to), disconnect the solar input breaker first to cut panel power.

Quick-Reference Checklist Before Using Heavy Power

  • [ ] Inverter unit switch ON?
  • [ ] Appliance switch (Stove/Outlets/Water Heater) ON?
  • [ ] Battery level via SOK app above 10%-20%?
  • [ ] If alarm sounds, stop and reduce load immediately.
  • [ ] When finished, turn appliance switches then inverter OFF.

Bathroom: Shower and Toilet

Shower

Your shower is a handheld sprayer located in the extra space above the passage to the driver's side. A shower curtain is stored there too.

Step-by-step use:

  1. Make sure the water pump is ON. The manual pump switch labled P (above the seating area, left of the battery board) must be on. You'll hear the pump hum briefly when it builds pressure.
  2. Turn on the water heater. The switch is on the small switchboard next to the seating area (labelled "Water Heater"). Wait 5–10 minutes for the water to warm up. (Don't forget to turn inverter on for this)
  3. Check the fresh water tank. Near that same switchboard is a tank level indicator. It's not accurate — when it reads "empty," there's usually still about a quarter tank left. Don't panic, but plan to refill soon.
  4. ⚠️ Never turn the water heater on if the fresh tank is completely dry. If unsure, refill first. Running the heater empty may burn out the element.
  5. Take out the composting toilet The compostiong toilet comes out. Unplug the vent pipe and chrod from the left side of the toilet and lift it up and out of the shower area.
  6. Hang up the curtains As you can see, directly above the shower there are 4 hooks. Hang the shower cutains on the hooks with the opening towards the sink.
  7. Put sprayer on Unscrew the tip of your faucet. it holds a small black rubber ring. place the ring on the tip of the spayer hose and screw it where the tip once was.
  8. Next to the faucet, you'll find hot and cold handles. Mix them to your preferred temperature just like at home.
  9. When done, replace the tip with the rubber ring hang the sprayer back, and let the curtain dry before storing.

Note: there is another rubber disk on the curtain hanger, if original is lost.

Draining: The shower water drains through a hole directly to the outside — it does not go into a holding tank. Be mindful of where you park; avoid letting water pool under the caravan on hard surfaces.

Ventilation: There is a ceiling vent fan above the bed and a window in the sliding door. After showering, open the vent fan and/or window to let moist air escape, it isn't required as there is a moisture catcher underneath the fire alarm.

Toilet (Composting)

The toilet hides under a counter cover. Lift the whole cover up to reveal the bathroom and toilet below.

How it works:

  • Front section = urine container (always open, no flap).
  • Rear section = solids bin, covered by a trap door that you open/close with a handle.

Daily step-by-step:

  1. Lift the counter cover up to access the bathroom.
  2. To pee, use the front section. No flushing needed.
  3. To poop:
    • Use the handle father from the wall to open the solids trap door.
    • Do your business.
    • Use the handle on the closer side to the wall to stir the solids a few times. This mixes the waste with the coir and aids composting.
    • Close the trap door with the handle.
  4. Toilet paper always goes in the trash bin, never into the toilet. There's a TP holder and the trash bin under the sink. This keeps the compost healthy and prevents jams.

The vent fan runs continuously. A small fan is built into the toilet system and stays on all the time (12V, powered by the batteries). It draws air through the toilet and exhausts it outside — this is what keeps the bathroom completely odor-free. Do not turn disconnect it needlessly.

If urine container smells pour a small amount of vineger in.

After use, lower the counter cover back down. The bathroom is hidden and the space is usable again.

Water System – Full Guide

This covers the complete water loop: filling fresh water, using the pump, dealing with grey water, and the critical cold-weather reality.

Fresh Water Tank & Filling

  • Location: Under the bed, on the right side.
  • Capacity: Approx. 40 gallons — generous for off-grid living.
  • Connection: Hose-thread fitting with a white cap. The fill hose is stored right next to it.

Step-by-step fill:

  1. Remove the white cap from the tank inlet.
  2. Take the hose head (with the red nozzle) and connect it to a potable (drinking water safe) garden hose — the ones marked "Potable". Never use Non-Potable.
  3. Connect the other end of the hose head to the tank inlet.
  4. Turn on the water supply at the spigot.
  5. Listen as the water flows. Fill until water nears the top, then STOP before it overflows. There's no automatic shut-off. Overfilling won't be good.
  6. Disconnect, replace the white cap, and store the hose. Make sure cap is well fastened and drain the hose as it may still hold some water. Use gravity to drain it.

Water Heater

  • Type: Electric only, powered by 120V (requires inverter ON).
  • Switch: On the small switchboard next to the seating area, labelled "Water Heater."
  • Wait time: 5–10 minutes.
  • ⚠️ Never turn the water heater on if the fresh tank is empty. Running it dry may destroy the element.
  • Tank level indicator: Next the switchboard, push the button left of the indicator to see level. Inaccurate — when it reads "empty," there's often a quarter tank left, but don't push it. Refill when it drops to empty for a few days.

Water Pump

  • Switch: One of the three manual switches above the seating area (labelled "P").
  • How it works: When the pump switch is ON, the pump pressurizes the lines. You'll hear it run briefly when you open a tap — that humming is normal. If it cycles on/off rapidly with taps closed, you might have a leak or airlock (see Troubleshooting).
  • Filter: The cold water line goes through an inline filter. The hot water line does not. If water flow slows or the pump strains, the filter may be partially clogged.
  • Pipe routing: Pipes run from the back of the caravan, inside the walls, to the sink.

Grey Water (Sink Drain)

This is not a holding tank in the traditional sense. Under the sink, there's a Lowe's bucket with a hole in it. That's your grey water receptacle.

  • It catches water from the sink drain.
  • Empty it regularly — pull the bucket out from the back door behind the sink and dump it every fe days. Pour onto the ground or into a proper disposal facility depending on local rules. Avoid dumping soapy water into fragile ecosystems.
  • Shower drains directly outside through a separate hole — does not go into this bucket.
  • No level indicator — check it manually and often.

⚠️ Cold Weather & Freezing — Critical

Honest truth: This caravan's water system is not heavily insulated, and there's no built-in winterization. Freezing temperatures will stop the water and can crack pipes. Be proactive.

Living in cold weather:

  • If outside temperatures drop below -15C:
    • Use the hot water line first if the cold side gels.
    • If no water comes from either tap, STOP. Don't force the pump. You may burn it out or split a frozen pipe. Warm the caravan thoroughly with the cabin heater and wait.
  • The pipes are inside the walls from the back, somewhat shielded but not insulated enough for a deep freeze.
  • There's no bypass valve or antifreeze system — if a prolonged deep freeze is expected, consider adding RV antifreeze to the fresh tank and pumping it through the lines, or just used bottled...

Sanitization (optional): If you notice odors or algae, you may add 1/4 cup household bleach per 15 gallons of water, let it sit, then thoroughly flush and drain. Not required with clean source water. We never did this. do at your own risk

Structure: Where Everything Is

Electrical – Component Locations

Think of the electrical system as a chain that starts under the bed, runs to a control area, and then feeds everything else.

Main Power Hub (Under the Bed – Left Side):

  • Inverter and two house batteries sit here (inverter behind, batteries in front).
  • Shore power connection and solar panel connection attach to the batteries at the side, with a breaker on the wall nearby.
  • Main fuse — behind the inverter. If the inverter loses all power, check here.

From the hub, power lines run to a circuit distribution box under the bench (seating area). That box then feeds the fridge, water pump, and — via the inverter - the stove, outlets and water heater if on.

Control Panel Area (Above the Seating Area):

  • Inverter ON/OFF button — master switch.
  • Battery switchboard — your fuel gauge (bars/percentage, not super accurate).
  • Three manual switches to the left of the battery switchboard:
    1. H - Heater ON/OFF (cabin heater master)
    2. P - Water pump
    3. L - Kitchen light

Heater Temperature Controls: Located next to the passage to the driver's cab. Set desired warmth here; the master heater switch above seating must also be ON.

Light Dimmer: Local switch with dimmer next to the sink — use for daily on/off and brightness adjustment.

Switchboard Next to Seating Area:

  • Small panel with switches for Water Heater, Outlets, and Stove.
  • Also houses the fresh water tank level indicator (inaccurate — reads empty with ~quarter remaining).

Storage System

All storage spaces, so nothing stays lost:

  • Under the bed (two shelves): The bottom shelf holds the fire extinguisher and the "husky bag" (tools/spares). Keep accessible. The upper shelf is free for personal items.
  • Kitchen drawers: Pull-to-open drawers under the cooktop and sink. For utensils, cookware, dry goods. If a drawer won't stay shut, the latch may be broken (see Maintenance).
  • Overhead cabinets: One row along the wall, mounted high and connected to the ceiling, above the kitchen, seating area and bed. For light items. Always make sure doors are closed before driving.
  • Under the seating area: The bench lifts up — deep compartment that also houses the circuit box. Store heavier items here.
  • Extra cabinet next to seating area: Standalone cabinet for quick-access items (first-aid kit, manuals, charging station).

Extras (Tools, Safety, and Important Bits)

Everything you need beyond the built-in systems:

  • Fire extinguisher: On the bottom shelf under the bed, right next to the husky bag. Know exactly where it is. Check the pressure gauge regularly — replace if the needle is in the red or if it's been used.
  • Husky bag: Heavy-duty tool bag on the same shelf. Contains items specifically for fixing and replacing house components:
    • Spare fuses (including inverter main fuse size)
    • Pliers (for fridge wheel and general use)
    • Screwdrivers (flathead and Phillips)
    • Small spare parts (latch blocks, screws, wire connectors)
    • Etc...

Before every trip: Check the fire extinguisher is accessible and the husky bag isn't buried under luggage. Easy access matters in an emergency.

Maintenance: Quick and Not So Quick Fixes

Emptying the Composting Toilet

How to prepare the coconut coir (drying medium):

You'll have compressed coconut coir bricks in the back. Never use them dry and crumbly — they must be properly rehydrated first.

  1. Place one brick in the solids compartment.
  2. Cover completely with water and let it sit for 10 minutes. The brick will expand.
  3. Use your hands to mix and break apart the coir until all fibers are separated. No hard chunks should remain.
  4. The finished coir should feel dry to the touch, or just barely moist. If too wet, let it air out or squeeze out excess water. Too much moisture causes smells.

Emptying urine:

  • Check every 1–2 days with regular use — the urine tank fills fastest, we use flashligt to the top of the container.
  • Remove the urine container, carry to a public toilet, vault toilet, or dump station, and pour it down. Rinse with a little water if available and if needed.

Emptying solids and cleaning:

  • The solids bin lasts 3–6 weeks for one or two people. Empty when the pile approaches the top or a faint odor appears (when working correctly, there should be virtually no smell).
  • Take the solids container to a vault toilet (pit toilet) or designated composting dump. Empty everything into the hole. Do not put in regular trash unless fully dry and local laws permit.
  • Clean the poop bowl (inside of solids container) each time you empty it if you'd like. Use water and mild soap if needed.
  • After emptying and cleaning, add Coconut coir (see section above) directly into the solids bin.
  • Latch care: No regular wiping needed unless you see visible poop on the closing latch. If you do, clean just that spot with soap and water.
  • Only use coconut coir (or approved medium). Never use soil, cat litter, or chemicals — they will ruin the composting process and may damage the toilet.

Fixing Inverter Issues

The inverter (Aims Power) sits in the back compartment, right next to the batteries.

If the inverter stops working (no power at outlets):

  1. Check the power switch — make sure the inverter is turned on. It's easy to bump the switch off.
  2. Press the reset button — a small round push-button on the inverter itself. If the inverter was overloaded, this may trip. Press firmly to reset.
  3. Check the main fuse behind the inverter — connected to the main power cable at behind the unit. You may need a flashlight to see it. If blown, replace with exactly the same amperage — never use a higher rating (fire risk). Keep spare fuses on hand (avaliavle in husky bag).

If none of these steps work, you may need a technician. But in our experience, it's almost always the reset button or that main fuse.

Fixing Drawers (and Fridge Slide)

Fridge Wheel Off-Track:

  • The fridge sits on a sliding tray with wheels that roll along a track. If overloaded or pulled sideways, a wheel can pop out.
  • Temporary fix: Use pliers to grip the axle of the misaligned wheel and twist it while guiding the wheel back into the track. Wiggle the fridge slightly to help it seat.
  • Prevention: Keep the fridge weight reasonable and always slide it straight and gently. Always lock the frige latches before driving a sharp turn will cause the fridge to come out.

Kitchen Drawers Won't Stay Closed:

  • The drawers use a simple pull-to-open plastic latch. The metal flap that holds them shut can break if pulled too hard or if the drawer slams open during travel.
  • Immediate fix: Slide the drawer back into place. It may stay while stationary, but will likely open during driving.
  • Proper fix: Remove the broken latch glue/screw the metal back in. the wood above it may be old, concider replacing it with a new wood block of same dimentions.
  • Until fixed: Use a bungee cord or childproof latch to keep the drawer shut during travel.
